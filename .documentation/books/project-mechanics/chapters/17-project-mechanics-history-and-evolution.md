# Project Mechanics History & Evolution

I started Project Mechanics in 2002 with one belief: that a repeatable methodology could reduce project chaos. Over the next seventeen years, that belief was tested by every market shift I encountered — the rise of social media, the enterprise SharePoint wave, the API economy, and the eventual obsolescence of the platform I'd built it all on. What follows isn't a product changelog. It's a record of the decisions I made, why I made them, and what I learned when they didn't go the way I expected. If you're building a practice around methodology, the arc of this history matters more than any single feature I added or dropped.

## Evolution Timeline

### 2004 — Foundation & Early Structure

I launched the first real version of the Project Mechanics site in December 2004, though the copyright traced back to 2002 when the thinking started. The structure was deliberately minimal — Home, Project Mechanics, Guestbook, Site Map. I believed at the time that simplicity would signal clarity of purpose. What I actually had was an empty vessel with good intentions. The guestbook, in hindsight, was my first attempt at community building, and it reflected a genuine question I was wrestling with: was Project Mechanics a methodology I'd share freely, or a service I'd sell? I didn't answer that question cleanly in 2004. That ambiguity drove the next several years.

### 2006 — Partnership with DeNovaCo

From February to August 2006, I operated the site under DeNovaCo branding with a significantly expanded navigation that reached into lifestyle categories — Offices, Adventure, Organizing, Living, Home Improvements, Fitness. I thought hosting under a partner brand would accelerate adoption and broaden the audience. The lifestyle expansion was a deliberate attempt to make project management principles feel accessible to people who would never call themselves project managers. What I found was that the breadth diluted the identity. Visitors couldn't tell what Project Mechanics actually was anymore, and neither, honestly, could I. The partnership created distribution but cost me focus. That's a trade-off I've thought about on many client engagements since: reach versus coherence.

### 2008 — Independent Operations Return

By September 2008, I'd decided the DeNovaCo arrangement wasn't working. The brand dilution cost more than the distribution gain was worth, and I returned to operating as Project Mechanics LLC. I also made a deliberate choice to put myself forward as the face of the work — case studies, portfolio websites, a visible founder. The lesson I took from the DeNovaCo period was direct: in services, the person is the brand. I also launched web publishing services during this phase, which felt like a natural extension of what I was already doing to maintain my own platform. Early social media integration appeared here too, though I was cautious about it. I'd seen enough hype cycles by 2008 to know that presence on a platform isn't the same as value from it.

### 2010 — Integrated Web Publishing Focus

Between November 2010 and early 2011, I repositioned around what I called "Integrated and Distributed Web Publishing." I added a real-time Twitter feed and Facebook Connect integration, and leaned into Web 2.0 framing. The Twitter integration reflected a genuine belief at the time that real-time engagement would differentiate the practice and help clients see Project Mechanics as current. What I learned later was that it distracted from the core signal. Visitors were watching my tweet stream when they should have been reading about methodology. The social media presence looked active; it just wasn't moving the work forward. That distinction — activity versus progress — is one I now surface explicitly in client engagements.

### 2011 — Microsoft SharePoint Integration

SharePoint integration became a major service offering starting in May 2011 because enterprise clients were asking for it. I built tools and frameworks to help organizations adopt SharePoint within the Project Mechanics methodology, and added the Web Project Mechanics platform concept alongside it. I also prominently featured my PMP certification during this period, partly because the enterprise clients expected it and partly because I wanted to signal that the SharePoint work was grounded in formal project management discipline, not just technical implementation. This phase opened a genuinely lucrative market segment. It also complicated the practice. I had to choose whether to own that complexity long-term — becoming a SharePoint consultancy — or treat it as one capability among several. I chose the latter, which shaped everything that followed.

### 2013 — Comprehensive Resource Hub

By 2013, I'd learned that feature accumulation was hurting usability. The site had grown dense with social feeds, analytics widgets, affiliate links, and content sections that each made sense in isolation but created noise together. I streamlined the design — removed the social media cruft that looked busy but delivered no client value — and rebuilt the navigation around what people actually needed: project management resources, leadership content, and clear pathways to services. I added Chartbeat for real-time analytics, which told me something uncomfortable: most visitors weren't engaging with the depth of content I'd built. They were skimming. That finding pushed me toward a content marketing framing for the 2013-2015 period — fewer pieces, better structured, more deliberately written. The Amazon affiliate bookstore I added during this phase was a small revenue experiment that I found more instructive as a signal of what visitors were interested in than as an actual income source.

### 2015 — Modern Responsive Design

By December 2015, the site structure I'd built wasn't working on mobile, and that was becoming an existential problem rather than an inconvenience. I rebuilt around responsive design with toggle navigation — not because it was technically exciting, but because I'd watched enough analytics to know that a growing share of my audience was on phones and tablets and bouncing immediately. I also added API development services during this period. The SharePoint wave had taught me that enterprise clients needed integration, not just methodology, and APIs were becoming the vocabulary for that integration. Requirements analysis and design sections got expanded attention as I tried to articulate the full arc of how I engaged with client projects — from problem definition through delivery.

### 2017 — Building Web APIs

In 2017, API development moved from a service I offered to a service I led with. The reasoning was practical: clients increasingly wanted to connect Project Mechanics methodology to their existing systems rather than adopt a new platform. APIs were the mechanism that made the methodology portable. I expanded the case studies section during this phase because I'd accumulated enough completed API work to show patterns, not just individual projects. The project management fundamentals stayed constant throughout — that was deliberate. The technical services were always meant to be expressions of the methodology, not replacements for it.

### 2019 — Technical Transition

The site became non-functional in early 2019, presenting only a JavaScript and cookies requirement message. What looked from the outside like a maintenance issue was actually the end of a platform era. The infrastructure I'd built the site on had reached the end of its useful life, and I made the decision not to rebuild on the same foundation. By this point, the methodology had been tested and documented across enough client engagements that it didn't need the website as its primary vehicle. The platform had served its purpose; what mattered was what I'd learned building and rebuilding it.

## What the Arc Actually Shows

What shaped this evolution wasn't a strategic roadmap — it was a sequence of pressures I responded to, some successfully and some not. The DeNovaCo partnership taught me that brand coherence matters more than distribution reach. The SharePoint pivot taught me that following client demand into enterprise markets creates capability but also creates complexity you have to actively manage. The 2013 streamline taught me that accumulation is the enemy of clarity, and that lesson applies as directly to methodology documentation as it does to website navigation. The shift to responsive design in 2015 was a reminder that the medium shapes whether the message reaches anyone at all. And the move to APIs in 2017 was, in retrospect, the most important insight of the entire arc: the methodology was portable; the platform wasn't.

Client demand pushed me toward enterprise services, and complexity followed. I learned that pulling back to clarity — in design, in service offering, in communication — was as important a move as any expansion. The through-line across all seventeen years wasn't any particular technology or partnership; it was the discipline of maintaining a coherent methodology while the tools and market around it kept changing. That discipline is what the next sections of this book are about.
