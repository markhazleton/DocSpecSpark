# The Managed Transition Model: Leadership Promotion as Power Exchange

Leadership promotions are often treated as celebratory milestones—an announcement, a title change, a new office. In reality, the most consequential aspect of promotion is not the event, but the transition of authority, identity, and responsibility that follows. I've watched enough promotions play out over the years to know that the celebration fades quickly, and what remains is the hard, often invisible work of actually becoming the leader the role demands.

The moment that sharpened this observation for me involved a VP of engineering I worked alongside who was promoted to CTO. He was technically brilliant—one of the best systems thinkers I'd encountered. But six months into the role, he was still writing code, still pulling late nights to push critical commits, still showing up to architecture reviews as a contributor rather than a decision-maker. The engineering teams were starved for strategic direction. Roadmap decisions drifted. Stakeholders escalated. The organization had promoted him into a leadership vacuum, and he had filled it by retreating to the work he already knew how to do. He eventually left. The failure wasn't competence—it was transition.

That pattern is what the Managed Transition Model is designed to prevent. Rather than viewing promotion as a singular step upward, the model reframes it as a coordinated exchange of power between the outgoing role and the incoming one. The framework distills what I've observed across multiple organizations into a structure with three elements that must work together:

- An internal push away from the past
- An external pull toward new authority
- A shared destination that aligns both forces

To visualize this, I find it useful to invoke Doctor Dolittle's Pushmi-Pullyu—a two-headed creature that must coordinate opposing forces to move forward.

When these elements synchronize, leadership transitions become smooth accelerators of organizational momentum. When they do not, friction, confusion, and stagnation follow.

### The Internal Push: Promotion Jettison

The first "head" of the Pushmi-Pullyu represents what I call Promotion Jettison—the disciplined act of releasing former duties to fully inhabit a new role.

Of the last six leaders I watched get promoted into larger roles, four failed to actually jettison within the first six months. All four eventually hit a ceiling where they couldn't scale because they were still solving the wrong-level problems. They were answering the wrong questions, attending the wrong meetings, and doing work that belonged to someone with a different title. The other two made deliberate, visible choices to release their prior duties early—and both went on to build teams that outperformed expectations in their first year. The difference wasn't intelligence or capability. It was willingness to let go.

> Promotion without jettison is not advancement. It is role duplication at a higher salary. The title changes, the salary changes, and the behavior stays the same.

#### Identity Lag

Leaders promoted for execution excellence often retain the self-image of a trusted doer rather than adopting the mindset of a strategic decision-maker. I've experienced this myself. There's a comfort in the work you know, and a vulnerability in the work you're still learning. I've seen this pattern so often I've started calling it identity lag: the title changes, expectations change, but the behavior does not.

Without intentional jettisoning, the leader remains psychologically anchored to yesterday's job. They keep attending meetings they no longer need, reviewing work they should be delegating, and solving problems that belong to someone else now. It feels productive, but it's actually a form of avoidance.

I'll add the counterpoint here, because it's real: there is a narrow window where refusing to let go is actually correct. In highly volatile situations—a production crisis, an acquisition in flight, a team in sudden disarray—the outgoing role holder sometimes needs to maintain continuity of expertise to prevent catastrophic execution drops. I've been in that position myself. But that exception is exactly that: an exception, and one that must be time-limited and made explicit. Most leaders I've watched use that rationale as a permanent excuse to avoid the discomfort of actually changing. The key is naming the exception deliberately, setting a hard end date, and treating it as a bridge rather than a destination.

#### The Competence Trap

High performers frequently confuse execution excellence with leadership readiness, and they interpret promotion as recognition for doing what they've always done, just at a higher level. I've watched this play out across engineering, finance, and product roles—the newly promoted leader doubles down on technical contribution precisely because that's where they feel competent, and competence feels like progress.

This creates what I think of as the Competence Trap: clinging to old strengths prevents development of new ones. The trap is subtle because the behavior looks like diligence from the outside. The promoted leader is working hard, delivering quality output, staying close to the details. But instead of scaling impact through leadership, they become a bottleneck disguised as a hero. The team learns to route everything through the new leader—not because it's efficient, but because the leader hasn't created space for others to own outcomes.

What I've found is that the leaders who escape this trap fastest are the ones who get uncomfortable on purpose. They deliberately remove themselves from decisions they could make easily, because they recognize that doing so is the only way to build the team's capacity to make those decisions without them. It doesn't feel like leadership in the moment. It feels like restraint. But restraint at this stage is the actual work.

#### Flyspeck Management

Failure to jettison often manifests as what I call Flyspeck Management—treating minor technical details with the same urgency as strategic decisions. I've seen this pattern derail otherwise talented leaders. They review every pull request, audit every report, approve every expense—not because any of these tasks require their attention, but because those are the tasks where they feel most competent.

The consequences accumulate:

- Slowed organizational velocity
- Disempowered team members
- Blurred accountability
- Leadership exhaustion

Promotion without jettison is not advancement. It is role duplication at a higher salary.

### The External Pull: "Rip It From My Hands"

If Promotion Jettison is the internal discipline, the second Pushmi-Pullyu head represents the external force that completes the transition: the successor—or the organization—must pull authority forward.

This is the accountability phase of leadership succession, and in my experience it's where organizations most often fail the promoted leader by doing nothing. The internal push alone is insufficient. A leader can want to let go, can intellectually commit to jettison, and still find themselves holding on—because no one on the other side is actually pulling. Transition requires two parties.

#### A Necessary Safety Net

When an outgoing leader remains trapped in old behaviors, the successor's demand for ownership becomes a protective mechanism for the organization. This isn't disrespect. It's organizational health. I've watched successors who were too deferential let promoted leaders linger in their old roles for months, waiting politely for authority that was never going to be handed over voluntarily. The successor has to claim the space.

Healthy transitions include:

- Explicit transfer of decision rights
- Visible ownership of outcomes
- Permission to lead differently

Without this pull, authority lingers in the past, and the organization stalls between two leaders—neither fully empowered, both partially accountable. What I've found is that the organizations that manage transitions well treat this pull as a formal expectation, not a personal negotiation. The successor isn't asking permission to lead. They're accepting authority that has already been assigned.

#### Nourishing vs. Toxic Dynamics

Allowing authority to be pulled forward is nourishing. It signals trust, validates readiness, and accelerates confidence. I've seen leaders who release authority gracefully elevated in the eyes of their organizations—not diminished. There's a paradox worth sitting with: the act of letting go strengthens your standing as a leader more than holding on ever could.

Blocking the pull is toxic. It breeds resentment, undermines legitimacy, and delays organizational learning. When a promoted leader can't stop doing their old job, the message to the successor is clear: *I don't trust you with this.* That message poisons the relationship and the team. I've watched capable successors lose confidence not because they were unready, but because they were never fully given the role they were told they had.

> Leadership maturity is revealed not in gaining power, but in releasing it well. The act of letting go strengthens your standing as a leader more than holding on ever could.

#### Define to Delegate

Authority cannot be transferred if responsibilities are unclear. The principle here is simple and direct: you cannot delegate what you cannot define. I've used this as a forcing function in my own work—when I struggle to explain a handoff clearly, it usually means I haven't thought clearly enough about what I'm actually giving away.

Clear scope, decision boundaries, and success measures transform an emotional transition into operational clarity. Before handing off work, a leader needs to articulate what success looks like, where the decision boundaries sit, and when escalation is appropriate. This isn't micromanagement—it's the scaffolding that makes autonomy possible. The successor needs enough definition to act confidently; without it, they'll either stall waiting for guidance or make decisions that feel unauthorized, even when they're not.

### The Destination: The Northbound Train

Push and pull alone are insufficient. I've seen transitions where both forces were present—the outgoing leader genuinely released authority, the successor genuinely claimed it—and the organization still fragmented, because the two parties were heading in different directions. Both forces must align toward a shared destination.

The clearest successful transition I've observed involved a director of product promoted to VP at a mid-sized software company. What made it work wasn't just that she jettisoned her prior portfolio—she did that deliberately within sixty days. And it wasn't just that her successor claimed ownership—he did, with encouragement from her. What made it work was that both of them were navigating toward the same destination: a specific customer-centered product strategy the executive team had defined explicitly before the transition started. Decisions that might otherwise have become territorial stayed clean, because the goal was shared. The role changed; the direction didn't.

That alignment is what I think of as the Northbound Train—an organization unified by clear philosophy, shared purpose, and a customer-centered definition of success. Without a Northbound destination, push becomes abandonment and pull becomes a power struggle. With it, transition becomes momentum.

#### Roles and Goals

Smooth succession requires separating two things that leaders often conflate:

- **Role**: Who I am in the organization
- **Goal**: What the organization must achieve

What I've noticed is that leaders who wrap their identity in a specific set of responsibilities will resist handing them over, even when it's clearly time—not because they're selfish, but because they've lost the distinction between the role and the goal. Clarifying that distinction produces collective progress. The goal persists even as the roles evolve. A leader who is oriented toward the destination can let go of the seat they occupied on the way there.

### Coordination Tools for Successful Transitions

Two tools have been particularly useful in my experience for synchronizing push, pull, and direction.

#### Six Honest Servingmen

Effective successors rapidly build organizational intelligence by asking six fundamental questions:

- **What** are the key deliverables and current commitments?
- **Where** do the critical dependencies and relationships sit?
- **When** are the milestones and decision points?
- **How** do the current processes and systems work?
- **Why** were certain decisions and trade-offs made?
- **Who** are the essential stakeholders and subject matter experts?

This structured curiosity converts uncertainty into informed authority. I encourage every new leader to run through these questions systematically during their first two weeks. The answers won't all come at once, but the discipline of asking builds credibility and accelerates understanding.

#### Stop, Start, Continue

During the first 90 days, clarity emerges through three straightforward questions:

- **Stop**: What legacy work must end?
- **Start**: What new leadership behaviors must begin?
- **Continue**: What must be preserved to sustain stability?

This framework operationalizes transition into visible progress. It also communicates to the team that the new leader is being deliberate rather than reactive. Change without a framework feels chaotic. Change guided by Stop/Start/Continue feels intentional. In practice, I've found that the "Stop" list is always the hardest—not because leaders don't know what to put on it, but because every item represents something they were once proud of doing.

### From Friction to Forward Motion

The Managed Transition Model reframes promotion from a personal achievement into an organizational system event. True advancement requires pushing away the past, pulling forward new authority, and aligning toward shared purpose. When these forces coordinate, leadership succession becomes predictable instead of chaotic, developmental instead of political, and accelerating instead of disruptive.

The harder truth I've arrived at after watching this play out repeatedly is that most promotion failures aren't failures of competence—they're failures of transition. The capability that earned the role is real. What breaks down is the willingness to stop being the person who had the previous role, and the organizational scaffolding that makes that letting-go possible and safe. That's what this model is designed to address.

Promotion, then, is not the moment someone receives power. It is the moment an organization successfully transfers motion. And like the Pushmi-Pullyu, progress only happens when both heads move together—northbound.
