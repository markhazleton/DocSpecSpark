# Project Mechanics Glossary

Essential terms and definitions for understanding and implementing the Project Mechanics methodology. Terms marked with ★ are signature concepts central to the Project Mechanics framework.

## A

### Accountability ★
Accountability is when an individual is the ultimate point of responsibility for the successful completion of a task or assignment. "The buck stops here." Accountability cannot be delegated — you can hand off the ability to act, but not the obligation to answer for the results. See also: *Authority*, *Authority-Accountability Matrix*.

### Authority ★
For decisions to be made, authority must be granted to individuals. Authority is the power that enables individuals to provide directions and structure to others. The levels of authority should be well defined before any project effort begins. Authority can be delegated. Accountability cannot. See also: *Authority-Accountability Matrix*, *Define to Delegate*.

### Authority-Accountability Matrix ★
A diagnostic framework with four quadrants defined by the intersection of authority and accountability levels: Leadership (high/high), Recklessness (high authority/low accountability), Frustration (low authority/high accountability), and Irrelevance (low/low). Effective governance keeps the people with accountability in the high-authority quadrant. See *Chapter 14*.

## B

### Benefits Realization
The process of tracking and measuring whether the projected business benefits of a project actually materialized after delivery. Most organizations approve projects based on projected benefits and never measure whether those outcomes were achieved. Benefits realization closes this loop.

### Blast Radius
The scope of potential impact if a change goes wrong. A key criterion in the hotfix readiness gate: a change with a small, well-understood blast radius is safer to deploy than one that could affect multiple systems or user flows. See *Chapter 13*.

## C

### Change Control
The formal process through which requested changes to project scope, schedule, or budget are reviewed, approved, and incorporated. Unmanaged change is scope creep. Managed change is a decision — made explicitly, with visibility into its impact. The discipline of change control is not about saying no; it's about making the cost of yes visible before it's accepted.

### Change Management
The organizational discipline of helping people transition from current to future state. Distinct from change control: change control manages project scope; change management manages human behavior. Technical projects produce outputs. Change management is what turns outputs into outcomes. See *Chapter 7*.

### Chargeability
The percentage of billable hours divided by a standard 40-hour work week. (40 hours billed = 100% chargeable, 20 hours = 50% chargeable.)

### Competence Trap
The leadership failure mode where a promoted individual clings to their former technical role rather than developing new leadership capabilities. High performers who defined their value through technical mastery find it difficult to let go of hands-on work, becoming bottlenecks disguised as heroes. See *Chapter 12*.

### Conference-Driven Development
A sizzle pattern in which a technology adoption is proposed primarily because someone attended a conference where it was prominently featured, rather than because it solves a specific organizational problem. See the *Sizzle Detection Model*, *Chapter 5*.

## D

### Define to Delegate
The principle that authority cannot be transferred if responsibilities are unclear. Before handing off work, a leader must articulate what success looks like, where the decision boundaries sit, and when escalation is appropriate. You cannot delegate what you cannot define. See *Chapter 12*.

## F

### Fast-Follow Hotfix Release ★
A release timing strategy for urgent fixes that protects an existing planned release from last-minute destabilization. Instead of injecting the fix into an upcoming release, the fix is deployed separately in a focused release window after dedicated regression testing. Protects the sprint release, allows clean rollback boundaries, and maintains delivery predictability. The recommended default path for most hotfix scenarios. See *Chapter 13*.

### Feature
A specific capability or functionality — a button, an algorithm, a system integration, a data export. Features are what a product *has*. They can be enumerated, scoped, and estimated. They're the language of sprints, backlogs, and release notes. Compare with *Outcome*.

### Feedback Failure ★
One of the Three Failure Modes. The project built what was specified but never measured whether it worked. Outcomes weren't tracked. Signals weren't monitored. Success was declared at delivery, not at impact. The most invisible of the failure modes: nobody can point to the moment it happened, because it's defined by the absence of something that should have been present. See *Chapter 0*.

### Flyspeck Management
The failure mode where a leader treats minor technical details with the same urgency as strategic decisions — reviewing every pull request, auditing every report, approving every small expense. A symptom of failure to execute *Promotion Jettison*. Named from Model-Netics Model #46. See *Chapter 12*.

## H

### Hotfix
An emergency fix deployed outside the normal release cycle in response to a production issue. Hotfixes carry elevated risk because they bypass the full testing cycle; the *Readiness Gate* and *Hotfix Prioritization Matrix* exist to make hotfix decisions deliberate rather than reactive. See *Chapters 13* and *15*.

## I

### Identity Lag
The leadership transition failure mode where a promoted individual retains the psychological identity of their former role despite a title change. The title changes; the behavior doesn't. A root cause of *Flyspeck Management* and *Promotion Jettison* failure. See *Chapter 12*.

## K

### Kickoff Meeting
The first formal gathering of the project team after project approval. The highest-leverage communication event in a project's life — where expectations are set, rules established, and the team's shared understanding of what they're doing begins to form. A kickoff that works covers: client expectations, team expectations, PM expectations, project rules, available resources, and project mechanics. It's a conversation, not a broadcast. See *Chapter 1*.

## N

### Northbound Train
A Model-Netics framework (Model #88) representing an organization unified by clear philosophy, shared purpose, and customer-centered definition of success. In a leadership transition, the Northbound Train provides the shared destination that aligns both the departing and arriving leader's decisions. See *Chapter 12*.

## O

### On-time
When deliverables are produced on the schedule communicated to the customer. This communicated time can be the original date or a mutually agreed-upon revised date.

### Outcome ★
The result a business wants to achieve — reduced support ticket volume, faster time to close a sale, higher user retention, lower operational cost. Outcomes are the language of strategy and business cases: what the business wants to be *different* after the project. A project that ships every feature on its list and produces no measurable improvement in the business has, in any meaningful sense, failed. Compare with *Feature*. See *Chapter 4*.

### Ownership Ambiguity ★
One of the Three Failure Modes. Nobody knows who makes the final call. Authority and accountability are out of alignment, and decisions get made in the wrong rooms by the wrong people. Often invisible until it becomes a crisis — by which point the cost of resolving it has compounded significantly. See *Chapter 0* and *Chapter 14*.

## P

### Project Charter
A document that states what the project will produce, what's explicitly out of scope, who is accountable for what, and what conditions would cause the project to be reconsidered or cancelled. A charter that can't be summarized in two paragraphs probably isn't well understood yet.

### Project End
A project ends when the mutually recognized solution has been reached, tested, reviewed, and finalized. Only after project-closing procedures have been completed does the project truly end.

### Project Life Cycle
The series of states a project moves through from inception to completion: Future/Opportunity, Project Definition, Proposed, Approved, In Progress, Complete, and Cancelled. The life cycle turns situational awareness into a discipline rather than an instinct. See *Chapter 1*.

### Project Management
The application of knowledge, skills, tools, and techniques to project activities to meet project requirements. Part art, part science. The science can be taught; the art is developed through experience and judgment.

### Project Mechanics ★
The set of repeatable, learnable behaviors that determine whether a project succeeds or fails, independent of its technical domain. The underlying structure: how you define and communicate scope, how you manage the people doing the work, how you make decisions under pressure, how you know when you're done.

### Project Start
A project begins when a particular need is identified and the necessity for further investigation of that need is authorized.

### Project Team
A temporary organization comprised of varying resources, guided by well-defined objectives and confined by a structure of cost, schedule, and quality parameters.

### Promotion Jettison
The disciplined act of releasing former duties to fully inhabit a new role after a promotion. The internal "push" in the Managed Transition Model. Without intentional jettisoning, the promoted leader remains psychologically anchored to yesterday's job. Named from Model-Netics Model #114. See *Chapter 12*.

### Psychological Safety
The team belief that members can speak up, take risks, and make mistakes without punishment. Not permissiveness — consistency in responding to honesty with problem-solving rather than blame. Teams without psychological safety don't bring problems early, which means the project learns about them when they're already expensive. See *Chapter 9*.

## Q

### Quality
To be considered a quality solution, a system must meet or exceed customer expectations with respect to its usability, scalability, and maintainability.

## R

### Readiness Gate ★
The non-negotiable set of verification criteria that must be satisfied before any hotfix enters a deployment path. Includes: confirmed root cause, code review completed, change scope bounded, tests passing, rollback plan defined, build artifact exists, and production monitoring ready. If any criterion is unchecked, the deployment conversation stops. See *Chapters 13* and *15*.

### Realization Rate
A dollar-per-hour measure of the ability to get paid for recorded hours, accounting for hours that had to be written off. Calculated by dividing total dollars paid by total hours charged.

### Responsibility
The obligation incurred by individuals in their organizational roles to effectively perform their assignments. Distinct from accountability: responsibility can be shared; accountability is singular.

## S

### Scope Creep
The uncontrolled expansion of project scope after baseline is established — typically through requirements added without going through the formal change control process. Teams that can't say no to late requirements don't fail because they're incompetent; they fail because they're responsive. The cure is a change control process that makes the cost of each addition visible before it's accepted.

### Scope Drift ★
One of the Three Failure Modes. The project started with a clear destination and arrived somewhere else. Requirements shifted without process; the new shiny feature displaced the original objective without anyone naming the trade-off. See *Chapter 0*. Compare: *Scope Creep* (uncontrolled change), *Sizzle* (novelty as driver of drift).

### Sizzle ★
What something looks like when the presentation is more compelling than the substance. In software: the demo that generates excitement, the framework that every blog post recommends, the architectural pattern that seems to solve everything at once. Sizzle generates momentum; the problem is when that momentum pulls a team away from the outcomes they were hired to deliver. See *Chapter 5*.

### Sizzle Detection Model ★
A three-question diagnostic for evaluating whether a proposed technology, architecture, or feature represents genuine value or sizzle. The three questions: (1) The Outcome Test — what specific, measurable outcome does this support? (2) The Alternative Test — is this the most effective path or just the most interesting? (3) The Evidence Test — what evidence, not intuition, supports this choice? See *Chapter 5*.

### Stakeholder Register
A document that identifies not just who the stakeholders are but what they care about and what would constitute success or failure from their perspective. The value is in surfacing competing definitions of success before they collide in execution.

## T

### Technical Debt
The accumulated cost of implementation shortcuts taken under time pressure or through insufficient design. Technical debt isn't inherently bad — sometimes the shortcut is the right call — but it must be acknowledged, tracked, and eventually addressed. Unacknowledged technical debt is the raw material of future emergencies.

### Three Constituencies ★
The three groups whose expectations must be actively managed on every project: the Client (who defines success and receives the output), Staff (who do the work and think in terms of feasibility and clarity), and Management (who resource the project and measure in budget and timeline terms). Each group speaks a different language, measures success differently, and will create conflict if their expectations are left unmanaged. See *Chapter 1*.

### Three Failure Modes ★
The three root causes behind every project failure: Ownership Ambiguity (unclear accountability, misaligned authority), Scope Drift (requirements shifted without process, novelty over substance), and Feedback Failure (outcomes not measured, signals not monitored). Most project failures involve all three in combination. See *Chapter 0*.

## U

### Utilization
The percentage of total non-administrative hours against a standard work period. Measures how much productive work a team member is doing, excluding administrative, sick, and vacation time.

## W

### Within Budget
A project is within budget when costs do not exceed the previously agreed-upon amount for delivery of the solution.

### Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)
A hierarchical decomposition of the project's deliverables into work packages small enough to estimate with confidence. WBS discipline catches the gaps that high-level plans miss — integration work, documentation, testing, the things that somehow never appear in the initial estimate because they feel like overhead until they become critical path.
